Content Area A: Cells & Tissues

A1.1 Prokaryotic Cells (Bacteria) ▼
Key organelles and structures:
  • Nucleoid: Region containing circular DNA (not membrane-bound)
  • Plasmids: Small circular DNA molecules, can carry antibiotic resistance genes
  • 70S ribosomes: Smaller than eukaryotic ribosomes, site of protein synthesis
  • Capsule: Protective layer outside cell wall, helps bacteria evade immune system
  • Cell wall: Made of peptidoglycan, maintains shape and prevents bursting
  • Cytoplasm: Gel-like substance where chemical reactions occur
  • Plasma membrane: Controls entry and exit of substances
  • Mesosomes: Infoldings of membrane, involved in respiration
  • Flagella: Tail-like structures for movement
  • Pili: Hair-like structures for attachment and DNA transfer
Gram-positive vs Gram-negative bacteria:
  • Gram-positive: Thick peptidoglycan cell wall, stains purple, more susceptible to antibiotics
  • Gram-negative: Thin peptidoglycan layer with outer membrane, stains pink/red, more antibiotic resistant
💡 Application Tip:
Prokaryotic cells are much smaller and simpler than eukaryotic cells. No membrane-bound organelles!
A1.1 Eukaryotic Cells (Animal & Plant) ▼
Organelles common to both animal and plant cells:
  • Plasma membrane: Controls what enters/exits cell
  • Cytoplasm: Site of chemical reactions
  • Nucleus: Contains genetic material (DNA) in chromosomes, controls cell activities
  • Nucleolus: Inside nucleus, makes ribosomes
  • Rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER): Studded with ribosomes, synthesizes and transports proteins
  • Smooth endoplasmic reticulum (SER): Synthesizes and processes lipids
  • Golgi apparatus: Modifies, packages and transports proteins, makes lysosomes
  • Vesicles: Membrane-bound sacs for transport
  • Lysosomes: Contain digestive enzymes, break down worn-out organelles and pathogens
  • 80S ribosomes: Site of protein synthesis (larger than prokaryotic)
  • Mitochondria: Site of aerobic respiration, produces ATP
  • Centrioles: Involved in cell division (animal cells)
  • Cilia: Hair-like structures for movement
Plant cell specific organelles:
  • Cell wall: Made of cellulose, provides strength and support
  • Chloroplasts: Site of photosynthesis, contain chlorophyll
  • Vacuole: Large permanent vacuole, stores cell sap, maintains turgor pressure
  • Tonoplast: Membrane surrounding vacuole
  • Amyloplasts: Store starch
  • Plasmodesmata: Channels connecting adjacent plant cells
  • Pits: Gaps in cell wall for transport
💡 Application Tip:
Plant cells have cell wall, chloroplasts and large permanent vacuole - animal cells don't!
A1.2 & A1.5 Recognizing Cells and Magnification ▼
Identifying organelles:
  • Must be able to identify organelles from electron micrographs and photomicrographs
  • Look for characteristic features: mitochondria (double membrane, cristae), chloroplasts (grana), nucleus (nuclear envelope)
Magnification calculations:
Magnification = Image size ÷ Actual size
Actual size = Image size ÷ Magnification
Image size = Actual size × Magnification
Example: Calculate magnification
Image of cell is 50 mm, actual size is 0.05 mm
Magnification = 50 ÷ 0.05 = ×1000
Example: Calculate actual size
Image is 40 mm, magnification is ×2000
Actual size = 40 ÷ 2000 = 0.02 mm = 20 μm
💡 Application Tip:
Make sure all measurements in SAME units before calculating! Convert mm to μm: multiply by 1000.
A2 Specialised Eukaryotic Cells ▼
Plant cells:
  • Palisade mesophyll: Packed with chloroplasts for photosynthesis, located near upper surface to absorb maximum light
  • Root hair cells: Long projection increases surface area for water and mineral absorption
  • Xylem cells: Dead hollow tubes with lignified walls for water transport, no end walls for continuous flow
  • Phloem cells: Sieve tube elements (living, no nucleus) and companion cells, transport sugars (translocation)
Animal cells:
  • Sperm cells: Tail for swimming, many mitochondria for energy, acrosome with enzymes to penetrate egg
  • Egg cells: Large with food reserves (cytoplasm), jelly coat to prevent polyspermy
  • Erythrocytes (red blood cells): Biconcave shape for large surface area, no nucleus for more hemoglobin, carry oxygen
  • Leucocytes (white blood cells): Have nucleus, phagocytes engulf pathogens, lymphocytes produce antibodies
  • Thrombocytes (platelets): Cell fragments involved in blood clotting
  • Neurones: Long axon for transmitting impulses, dendrites to receive signals, myelin sheath for insulation
💡 Application Tip:
Link structure to function! Each specialization helps the cell perform its specific role more effectively.
A3 Epithelial Tissue ▼
Squamous epithelium:
  • Flat, thin cells arranged in single layer
  • Location: Alveolar epithelium in lungs
  • Function: Very thin for efficient gas exchange by diffusion
  • Short diffusion distance for oxygen and carbon dioxide
Columnar epithelium:
  • Tall, column-shaped cells
  • Goblet cells: Produce mucus to trap pathogens and particles
  • Ciliated cells: Have cilia that waft mucus upwards away from lungs
  • Function: Protect lungs from infection
COPD (Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease):
  • Damage to epithelial tissue in airways
  • Cilia destroyed, mucus not removed, increased infections
  • Alveoli walls break down, reduced surface area for gas exchange
  • Causes: Smoking, air pollution
💡 Application Tip:
Understand how tissue damage leads to symptoms - destroyed cilia mean mucus builds up causing infections.
A3 Endothelial, Muscular & Nervous Tissue ▼
Endothelial tissue (in blood vessels):
  • Single layer of flat cells lining blood vessels
  • Smooth surface reduces friction for blood flow
  • Atherosclerosis: Damage to endothelium → fatty deposits build up → narrows arteries → increases blood pressure, risk of heart attack/stroke
  • Risk factors: High blood pressure, smoking, high cholesterol, lack of exercise
Muscular tissue (skeletal muscle):
  • Contains myofibrils made of actin and myosin filaments
  • Fast-twitch fibres: Contract rapidly, tire quickly, use anaerobic respiration, for short bursts (sprinting)
  • Slow-twitch fibres: Contract slowly, resist fatigue, use aerobic respiration, many mitochondria, for endurance (marathon)
Nervous tissue:
  • Myelinated neurones: Covered in myelin sheath, faster impulse transmission (saltatory conduction), impulse jumps between nodes of Ranvier
  • Non-myelinated neurones: No myelin, slower impulse transmission, impulse travels along whole axon
Nerve impulse transmission:
  • Resting potential: Inside neurone negative relative to outside (-70mV)
  • Action potential: Sodium ions rush in (depolarization), then potassium ions move out (repolarization)
  • Changes in membrane permeability to Na⁺ and K⁺ ions
Synaptic transmission:
  • Impulse arrives → calcium ions enter → vesicles release neurotransmitter
  • Neurotransmitter diffuses across synaptic cleft
  • Binds to receptors on postsynaptic membrane
  • Triggers new impulse in next neurone
Parkinson's disease:
  • Caused by lack of dopamine (neurotransmitter) in brain
  • Symptoms: Tremors, slow movement, stiffness
  • Drug treatments: Agonists (mimic dopamine), antagonists (block receptors), precursors (converted to dopamine)
💡 Application Tip:
Know the difference between types of fibres and neurones, and be able to explain why each is suited to its function.

Content Area B: Biological Molecules

B1 Structure & Function of Water ▼
Structure:
  • Chemical formula: H₂O
  • Contains hydrogen (H) and oxygen (O) atoms
  • Covalent bonds within water molecule
  • Polar molecule (δ+ on H, δ- on O)
  • Hydrogen bonds form between water molecules
Functions:
  • Solvent: Dissolves ionic and polar substances, called "universal solvent"
  • Medium for reactions: Chemical reactions occur in aqueous solution
  • Transport: Transports dissolved substances in blood, xylem, phloem
  • pH regulation: Helps maintain constant pH
  • Electrolyte balance: Maintains concentrations of ions in body fluids
  • Temperature regulation: High specific heat capacity, stable temperature; evaporation of sweat cools body
  • Cohesion-tension: Water molecules stick together, allows continuous column in xylem for transpiration
💡 Application Tip:
Water's polarity makes it an excellent solvent for ionic and polar substances - essential for life!
B2 Structure & Function of Carbohydrates ▼
Carbohydrates: Organic molecules containing carbon, hydrogen and oxygen (general formula CₓH₂ₓOₓ)
Monosaccharides (single sugar units):
  • α-glucose and β-glucose: Isomers, 6-carbon sugars (hexoses)
  • Galactose: 6-carbon sugar
  • Fructose: 6-carbon sugar, found in fruits
  • Ribose and deoxyribose: 5-carbon sugars (pentoses), in RNA and DNA
Disaccharides (two monosaccharides joined):
  • Lactose: Glucose + galactose (milk sugar)
  • Maltose: Glucose + glucose (malt sugar)
  • Sucrose: Glucose + fructose (table sugar)
  • Formed by condensation reaction (removes H₂O)
  • Glycosidic bond formed between monosaccharides
Polysaccharides (many monosaccharides joined):
  • Starch (amylose & amylopectin): Energy storage in plants, made of α-glucose, insoluble, compact
  • Glycogen: Energy storage in animals (liver, muscles), highly branched α-glucose chains
  • Cellulose: Structural support in plant cell walls, made of β-glucose, straight chains with hydrogen bonds
Functions:
  • Energy release: Glucose respired to produce ATP
  • Energy storage: Starch (plants), glycogen (animals)
  • Structural: Cellulose in cell walls
Tests for carbohydrates:
  • Benedict's test (reducing sugars): Add Benedict's reagent, heat. Blue → brick red precipitate = positive
  • Iodine test (starch): Add iodine solution. Yellow/brown → blue/black = positive
💡 Application Tip:
Remember the difference: α-glucose makes starch/glycogen (storage), β-glucose makes cellulose (structure).
B3 Structure & Function of Proteins ▼
Proteins: Polymers made of amino acid monomers joined by peptide bonds
Structure levels:
  • Primary structure: Sequence of amino acids in polypeptide chain, joined by peptide bonds (formed by condensation)
  • Secondary structure: Folding of chain into α-helices or β-pleated sheets, held by hydrogen bonds
  • Tertiary structure: 3D folding of polypeptide, held by:
    • Hydrogen bonds
    • Ionic interactions (between charged R groups)
    • Disulfide bridges (between sulfur atoms in cysteine)
    • Van der Waals forces
  • Quaternary structure: Multiple polypeptide chains joined together (e.g., hemoglobin has 4 subunits)
Classification:
  • Globular proteins: Compact, spherical, soluble (e.g., enzymes, antibodies, hemoglobin)
  • Fibrous proteins: Long, narrow, insoluble, structural (e.g., collagen, keratin)
Functions:
  • Muscles: Actin and myosin for contraction
  • Enzymes: Biological catalysts
  • Antibodies: Immune defense
  • Antigens: Cell recognition markers
  • Carrier proteins: Transport substances across membranes
  • Hormones: Chemical messengers (e.g., insulin)
  • Transport: Hemoglobin carries oxygen
  • Growth & repair: Build and repair tissues
  • Blood clotting: Fibrinogen → fibrin
Biuret test for proteins:
  • Add Biuret solution (sodium hydroxide + copper sulfate)
  • Positive result: Blue → purple/lilac color
💡 Application Tip:
The order of structure is ALWAYS: primary → secondary → tertiary → quaternary. Each level depends on the previous.
B4 Structure & Function of Nucleic Acids ▼
Nucleotide structure:
  • Three components: pentose sugar, phosphate group, nitrogenous base
  • DNA: Deoxyribose sugar
  • RNA: Ribose sugar
Nitrogenous bases:
  • Purines: Adenine (A), Guanine (G) - double ring
  • Pyrimidines: Cytosine (C), Thymine (T in DNA), Uracil (U in RNA) - single ring
DNA structure:
  • Double helix with two polynucleotide strands
  • Sugar-phosphate backbone on outside
  • Bases pair in middle: A with T (2 hydrogen bonds), C with G (3 hydrogen bonds)
  • Complementary base pairing
  • Antiparallel strands (run in opposite directions)
RNA structure:
  • Single polynucleotide strand
  • Contains uracil (U) instead of thymine
  • Shorter than DNA
Functions:
  • DNA: Stores genetic information in genes, instructions for making proteins
  • RNA: Transfers information from DNA to ribosomes for protein synthesis, controls gene expression
💡 Application Tip:
Remember complementary base pairing: A-T (DNA) or A-U (RNA), and C-G. Purines pair with pyrimidines.
B5 Structure & Function of Lipids ▼
Lipids: Organic molecules containing carbon, hydrogen and oxygen (less oxygen than carbohydrates)
Structure:
  • Made from fatty acids and glycerol
  • Saturated fats: No C=C double bonds, solid at room temperature (animal fats)
  • Unsaturated fats: Have C=C double bonds, liquid at room temperature (plant oils)
  • Triglycerides: 1 glycerol + 3 fatty acids, joined by esterification (condensation)
  • Diglycerides: 1 glycerol + 2 fatty acids
Functions:
  • Energy source: Provide more than twice the energy per gram than carbohydrates
  • Energy storage: Stored as adipose tissue
  • Insulation: Layer under skin reduces heat loss
  • Protection: Cushions organs
  • Cell membranes: Phospholipids form bilayer
  • Steroid hormones: E.g., testosterone, estrogen
Emulsion test for lipids:
  • Add ethanol to sample, shake, then add water
  • Positive result: Cloudy white emulsion forms
💡 Application Tip:
Lipids are hydrophobic (water-repelling). Phospholipids have hydrophilic heads and hydrophobic tails - perfect for membranes!

Content Area C: Transport & Enzymes

C1 Cell Membrane & Transport ▼
Fluid mosaic model of cell membrane:
  • Phospholipid bilayer: Hydrophilic heads face outwards, hydrophobic tails face inwards
  • Proteins: Intrinsic (span membrane), extrinsic (on surface)
  • Cholesterol: Adds stability and regulates fluidity
  • Glycoproteins & glycolipids: Cell recognition and signaling
  • "Fluid" because phospholipids can move, "mosaic" because of scattered proteins
Transport methods:
Method Energy? Description
Diffusion No (passive) Movement from high to low concentration down gradient
Facilitated diffusion No (passive) Through protein channels/carriers, down gradient
Osmosis No (passive) Diffusion of water through partially permeable membrane
Active transport Yes (ATP) Through carrier proteins, against gradient, requires ATP
Endocytosis Yes (ATP) Bulk transport into cell, membrane folds inwards
Exocytosis Yes (ATP) Bulk transport out of cell, vesicle fuses with membrane
Surface area to volume ratio:
  • As organism gets larger, SA:V ratio decreases
  • Small organisms: High SA:V, sufficient for diffusion alone
  • Large organisms: Low SA:V, need specialized transport systems (lungs, circulatory system)
  • Adaptations to increase SA: Villi in intestine, alveoli in lungs, root hairs
💡 Application Tip:
Passive transport needs NO energy, active transport needs ATP. Remember: Active = ATP!
C2 Enzymes as Biological Catalysts ▼
Enzyme: Biological catalyst that speeds up rate of reaction without being used up, made of protein
Active site: Region on enzyme with specific tertiary structure complementary to substrate
Substrate: Molecule that enzyme acts upon
Structure:
  • Globular proteins with specific tertiary structure
  • Active site has specific shape complementary to substrate
  • If shape changes (denaturation), enzyme no longer works
How enzymes work:
  • Collision theory: Enzyme and substrate must collide with correct orientation and sufficient energy
  • Lock and key theory: Active site is rigid, exactly complementary to substrate shape
  • Enzyme-substrate complex: Substrate binds to active site, forming ES complex
  • Products formed: Bonds broken/made, products released, enzyme unchanged and can be reused
  • Specificity: Each enzyme catalyzes one specific reaction due to unique active site shape
Measuring enzyme activity:
  • Initial rate of reaction: Rate at start when substrate concentration is high
  • Measure product formed OR substrate used per unit time
  • Graph: Product/time, steepest part at start = initial rate
💡 Application Tip:
Enzymes are NOT used up - they can be used again and again. They just speed up reactions that would happen anyway!
C2.3 Factors Affecting Enzyme Activity ▼
Temperature:
  • Low temp: Slow reaction, low kinetic energy, fewer successful collisions
  • Optimum temp: Fastest reaction rate (usually 37°C for human enzymes)
  • Above optimum: Enzyme denatures, active site shape changes permanently, enzyme stops working
pH:
  • Each enzyme has optimum pH
  • Too far from optimum: Hydrogen bonds break, active site shape changes, enzyme denatures
  • Examples: Pepsin (stomach) optimum pH 2, amylase optimum pH 7
Substrate concentration:
  • Low concentration: Rate increases as concentration increases, more collisions
  • High concentration: Rate plateaus, all active sites occupied, enzyme concentration is limiting factor
Enzyme concentration:
  • More enzyme = faster reaction (if substrate available)
  • Direct relationship: Double enzyme = double rate
  • Only if substrate concentration is not limiting
Example: Explain temperature effect on enzyme
Below optimum: Increase in temperature increases rate because molecules have more kinetic energy, more frequent successful collisions between enzyme and substrate.
Above optimum: Enzyme denatures because high temperature breaks hydrogen bonds in tertiary structure, changing active site shape so substrate no longer fits.
💡 Application Tip:
Denatured = permanently damaged. Remember: Temperature and pH extremes denature enzymes!
C3 Homeostasis ▼
Homeostasis: Maintenance of constant internal environment despite external changes
Purpose of homeostasis:
  • Maintain optimum conditions for enzyme action and cell function
  • Keep variables at set point (temperature, pH, blood glucose, water potential)
Components of control systems:
  • Stimulus: Change in internal/external environment
  • Receptors/sensors: Detect changes
  • Control centre: Processes information, coordinates response
  • Effectors: Muscles or glands that bring about response
  • Feedback: Information about level of variable
Negative feedback:
  • Most common type
  • Response reverses the change to restore normal level
  • Examples: Blood pressure, osmoregulation (body fluids), gas concentration (O₂/CO₂), blood sugar levels
Example: Blood glucose regulation (negative feedback)
High blood glucose: Detected by pancreas → insulin released → cells take up glucose → glucose stored as glycogen → blood glucose decreases
Low blood glucose: Detected by pancreas → glucagon released → glycogen broken down to glucose → blood glucose increases
Positive feedback:
  • Response enhances/amplifies the change
  • Less common, usually specific situations
  • Blood clotting: Platelets trigger more platelets to aggregate
  • Labour contractions: Oxytocin causes contractions, which stimulates more oxytocin release
💡 Application Tip:
Negative feedback = reverses change. Positive feedback = amplifies change. Most homeostasis uses negative feedback!
C3.4 Nervous & Endocrine System Interaction ▼
Autonomic nervous system:
  • Controls involuntary actions (breathing, heartbeat)
  • Not under conscious control
  • Works continuously
Adrenal glands (fight or flight response):
  • Release adrenaline hormone in stress/danger
  • Effects: Increases heart rate, breathing rate, blood glucose, diverts blood to muscles
  • Prepares body for action
Hypothalamus:
  • Link between nervous and endocrine systems
  • Regulates temperature, hunger, thirst
  • Controls pituitary gland (master gland)
Peripheral nervous system:
  • Relays information between body and central nervous system (CNS)
  • Sensory neurones carry impulses TO CNS
  • Motor neurones carry impulses FROM CNS to effectors
💡 Application Tip:
Nervous system = fast, short-lived, electrical impulses. Endocrine system = slower, longer-lasting, hormones in blood.
C3.5 Disturbance of Homeostasis ▼
Ageing effects:
  • Feedback loops become less effective
  • Heart failure: Heart unable to pump blood effectively
  • Type 2 diabetes: Cells become resistant to insulin, high blood glucose
Lifestyle influences on homeostasis:
  • Nutrition: Poor diet → obesity, diabetes, high blood pressure
  • Physical activity: Exercise improves cardiovascular health, reduces diabetes risk
  • Drug/alcohol abuse: Damages organs (liver, heart, brain), disrupts homeostasis, addiction affects nervous system
Consequences:
  • Body cannot maintain constant internal environment
  • Increased risk of disease
  • Reduced life expectancy
💡 Application Tip:
Lifestyle choices directly affect homeostasis. Healthy lifestyle = effective homeostasis = reduced disease risk!

Command Words

Critical Command Words - Exact Definitions ▼
⚠️ These definitions are from official Pearson documentation
Understanding these precisely is essential for exam success
Calculate

Obtain numerical answer, showing relevant working and appropriate units where required

Always show working. Always include units.
Compare

Look for similarities AND differences. Answer must relate to both/all things mentioned

Must discuss BOTH items. Use "whereas", "however", "in contrast".
Describe

Give an account of something. Statements need to be developed but don't need justification

Say WHAT happens, not WHY. No reasoning needed.
Explain

Requires justification/exemplification. Must contain reasoning. Mark scheme:

  • 1 mark: Identification of point
  • 2 marks: Identification + linked justification
  • 3 marks: Identification + two linked justifications OR further development
  • 4 marks: Two identifications with expansions OR one with three detailed points
Use "because", "this is due to", "as a result". Link statements.
Evaluate

Review information, bring together to form conclusion. Use evidence including strengths, weaknesses, alternatives. Make supported judgement

Consider multiple perspectives. Make judgements. Support with evidence.
Discuss

Identify the issue. Explore all aspects. Investigate by reasoning or argument. Does NOT require conclusion

Present multiple viewpoints. No single answer required.
Deduce

Draw/reach conclusion(s) from the information provided

Use the information given to work out the answer.
State what is meant by

Provide the accepted definition of a term

Use exact wording from specification/mark schemes.
Common Biology Exam Errors ▼
❌ Error #1: Insufficient detail in explanations

Students identify what happens but don't explain WHY.

✓ Fix: Always link your points with "because", "this causes", "as a result"

❌ Error #2: Confusing similar terms

E.g., prokaryotic/eukaryotic, DNA/RNA, diffusion/osmosis, mitosis/meiosis

✓ Fix: Learn precise definitions. Create comparison tables to see differences clearly.

❌ Error #3: Not linking structure to function

Describing structure without explaining how it helps the function.

✓ Fix: For every structure mentioned, explain how it helps perform the function.

❌ Error #4: Vague enzyme explanations

Saying "enzyme stops working" instead of "enzyme denatures"

✓ Fix: Use precise terminology: denature, active site, complementary, enzyme-substrate complex

❌ Error #5: Missing biological molecule tests

Forgetting reagents, colors, or conditions for Benedict's, Biuret, Iodine, Emulsion tests

✓ Fix: Make flashcards for each test: reagent → procedure → positive result color

❌ Error #6: Magnification calculation errors

Units not matching, wrong formula used, no working shown

✓ Fix: Convert ALL to same units first. Use triangle: Magnification = Image / Actual

❌ Error #7: Incomplete homeostasis descriptions

Missing parts of the feedback loop (receptor, control centre, effector)

✓ Fix: Always include: stimulus → receptor → control centre → effector → response

❌ Error #8: Not identifying cell types from images

Unable to recognize organelles in electron micrographs

✓ Fix: Practice with lots of images. Learn characteristic features of each organelle.
